The Anatomy of a Winning Proposal
A strong proposal for a small or medium freelance project has six parts. In order:

1. Opening: address the client's specific problem, not your credentials
2. Understanding: show you read the brief and understood what they actually need
3. Approach: how you will solve it, in plain language
4. Deliverables: exactly what you will hand them when the work is done
5. Price and timeline: specific numbers, not ranges
6. Call to action: one clear next step
7. For small projects (under KES 20,000 or USD 150), keep the whole proposal under 200 words. For larger projects, you can go up to 400 words. Never longer than that for an initial proposal.

The Opening That Wins vs The Opening That Loses

Loses
"Hello, I am a skilled Python developer with 3+ years of experience in web development, API integration, data analysis, machine learning and more. I have worked with many clients and I am confident I can deliver your project on time and within budget."

Wins
"You are spending too much time answering the same WhatsApp questions about prices and stock. I can automate those replies so your team handles only the complex ones."

The losing opening could have been written for any brief on the platform. Every developer in the applicant list has years of experience and is confident. Your first sentence must prove you read their specific brief.

The winning opening names their problem using language from their brief. It shows immediately that you understand what they need. That alone puts you ahead of 80% of applicants.

Cut Generic Sentences
Before you submit any proposal, read each sentence and ask: could my competition have written this sentence without reading this brief? If yes, cut it or rewrite it to be specific.

Generic sentences to cut:

"I am a dedicated and hard-working professional..."
"I always deliver quality work..."
"I am confident I can complete this project..."
"Reach out with any questions..."
"I look forward to working with you..."
Every word that is in your proposal should earn its place by adding specific value. If it does not, cut it.

Read your proposal out loud before you send it. If any part sounds like it was copied from a template, it was. The client can tell. Read it as if you are speaking directly to them about their specific situation.

A Complete Proposal Example
Using Brief 1 from yesterday (WhatsApp bot for agricultural inputs business):

Opening
You mentioned your team spends too much time answering repetitive WhatsApp messages about prices and stock. I can build an automated reply system that handles those common questions instantly, 24/7, so your team focuses only on conversations that actually need a human.

Understanding
Before building anything, I would want to understand three things: what platform you use for WhatsApp (WhatsApp Business app or the API), where your price and stock data currently lives, and whether you need the bot to take orders or just provide information.

Approach
I will set up an automated reply flow connected to your current product list. Customers asking about prices or availability get an instant, accurate response. Messages outside that scope get flagged for your team to handle manually.

Deliverables
A working automated reply system, a simple admin interface to update prices and stock, and a 30-minute handover call so your team knows how to use and maintain it.

Price and timeline
KES 35,000. Ready in 10 working days from when you provide access to your WhatsApp Business account and product list. 50% upfront, 50% on delivery.

Call to action
Happy to answer the three questions above before you decide. What platform do you currently use for WhatsApp?

Notice the proposal ends with a question, not with a generic "looking forward to working with you." A question invites a response. It starts a conversation. It shows you are already thinking about the project.

Handling the Questions You Identified Yesterday
Yesterday you wrote three clarifying questions. In a proposal, you have two choices with those questions:

Option 1: State your assumptions and include them in your price and timeline. "I am assuming the data lives in a Google Sheet. If it is a database, the timeline extends by 3 days." This shows you thought about the complexity and protects you from scope creep.

Option 2: Ask one question in the proposal and park the rest for the discovery call. The call to action above asked just one question. You save the rest for when they respond. Do not dump all your questions into the proposal.

Most of the time, Option 1 is better. It shows confidence and moves things forward without needing the client to respond before you can give a price.

Price and Timeline: Be Specific
Never give a range in your proposal. "KES 15,000 to 40,000" tells the client nothing and makes you look uncertain. If you genuinely do not have enough information to give a specific price, say so and ask the one question you need answered before you can quote.

For the timeline, always say "working days" not "days." State when the clock starts. "10 working days from when I receive access to your system." This protects you from delays on their side.
